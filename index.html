How Tommy John Has Changed Baseball

When Chasing Velocitty Changes Everything

Tommy John surgery has become very common amongst pitchers, not only professionals but even high schoolers. What was once considered a rare and career-threatening injury is now an increasingly common injury with pitchers not only being able to return but able to return with 1-2 calendar years since the injury.

The Rise of Tommy John

Using data collected by MLBPlayerAnalys, this graph examines the number of recorded Tommy John surgeries each year between 2000 and 2025.

Figure 1. Annual recorded Tommy John surgeries from 2000 to 2025. Source: MLBPlayerAnalys Tommy John Surgery List.

The data shows a substantial increase in recorded Tommy John surgeries over the past 25 years. In 2000, there were 29 recorded surgeries, compared with 176 in 2021, the highest year in the dataset. Although the number of surgeries fluctuates from year to year, Tommy John surgery has become increasingly common in professional baseball.

What Does This Mean for Baseball?

According to the American Medical Association, the share of active major-league pitchers who had undergone Tommy John surgery hit 35.3% in 2023. According to Baseball-Reference a total of 863 different pitchers appeared on the mound across the 30 Major League Baseball teams during the 2023 season. Meaning that along with the 35.3% of pitchers that already had tommy john, an additonal 16% had to get an inital or additonal surgery as well. For reference, before 2000, all 57 recorded pitchers to get TJ retired without every pitching again. As Tommy John surgeries have become increasingly common, one possible contributing factor is baseball's growing emphasis on pitching velocity. Over the past two decades, the average MLB fastball velocity has steadily increased, as teams increasingly prioritize pitchers who can throw harder and generate more strikeouts. While throwing harder can make pitchers more effective, it can also place greater stress on the elbow particularly the ulnar collateral ligament (UCL), which is commonly injured in pitchers who undergo Tommy John surgery. However, this emphasis on velocity does not stop at the major league level. Minor league, college, and even high school pitchers are facing growing pressure to throw harder in hopes of earning promotions, scholarships, or professional opportunities. With advanced technology such as radar guns and pitching analytics becoming more accessible, young athletes are increasingly focused on improving their velocity at earlier ages. This raises concerns about whether pitchers are prioritizing throwing harder over their long-term arm health. Although increasing velocity cannot be blamed entirely for the rise in Tommy John surgeries, the relationship between pitching performance, physical stress, and injury risk raises important questions about the future of baseball. As the sport continues to reward higher velocities, teams, coaches, and players must consider whether the pursuit of throwing harder is worth the potential consequences.
